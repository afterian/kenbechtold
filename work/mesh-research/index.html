Glossy surfaces: model the reflections

On shiny material, highlights slide across the surface as the view changes, so matched "features" are reflections, not geometry. Gaussian splats carry view-dependent colour, so reflections become appearance instead of false shape.

0.902 F-score for 2DGS on the glossy public boot, against 0.416 for the best normal-integration run.

    · Chapter I · Beyond photogrammetryThe limit
    I

    Photogrammetry
    needs texture.

    Classic photogrammetry finds the same tiny patch of texture in several photos and triangulates it. Thousands of matches become a point cloud, then a mesh.

    Smooth, uniform surfaces (moulded rubber, painted panels, coated leather) give it almost nothing to hold on to. On our featureless test object the classic solver didn't produce a sparse model at all.

    EvidenceCOLMAP 4.2.1 on the bear: 23–51 features per view, 0 verified image pairs. On the textured Lego: 584–3,039 features per view.

    · Chapter I · Beyond photogrammetryWhat we're solving for
    From pixels to a printable surface

    A photo knows colour.
    A product needs shape.

    A mesh is a skin of triangles: the format every 3D tool, renderer and printer speaks. Getting one from photographs means recovering what a camera never records directly: where each surface point sits and which way it faces.

    Shoes are adversarial: smooth panels, glossy materials, thin straps and hidden soles. So every method here is tested on public objects that come with a laser-scanned ground truth, and on our own scanner's captures.

    Three alternatives to matching texture

    Three families of method,
    one shared finish line.

    Road A reads shape from shading: the camera stays still while lights change. Road B fits Gaussians to many views and optimizes appearance instead of matching points. Road C predicts the cameras with learned models when there's nothing to match.

    · Chapter II · How it worksPhotometric stereo
    II

    Same camera,
    different light.

    One real camera view of the bear, photographed six times, each time lit by a single lamp. Nothing moves except the light. The glyph under each frame shows where that lamp sat.

    How brightly a patch glows depends on the angle between its surface and the lamp. Watch enough lamps and you can solve for that angle at every pixel, whether or not the surface has any texture. The answer is the surface normal. Modern AI models do this without being told where the lamps were.

    brightness = albedo × max(0, n · l)

    Move the pointer over the sphere to move the lamp.

    Real OLAT photographs: DiLiGenT-MV bear, view 01, lights 001/019/037/055/073/091. Glyphs use the dataset's calibrated light directions.

    · Chapter II · How it worksGaussian splatting & neural SDF
    Optimize appearance, not matches

    Many cameras, one light:
    fog first, surface second.

    These methods fit hundreds of thousands of soft, coloured blobs (“Gaussians”) until rendered views match the photos. Because they compare whole images rather than matching texture points, smooth regions still get constrained. The cloud isn't a surface yet: a second extraction step builds one, and that step decides much of the quality.

    · Chapter II · How it worksFinding the cameras
    Both roads need to know where each camera was

    Predict the cameras,
    then check them against the pixels.

    Classic structure-from-motion chains many fragile steps, each depending on matched texture. Feed-forward models look at all photos at once and output cameras in seconds. We never trust them blind: every prediction is checked against independent feature evidence.

    Case 02 · The phantom sheets

    Same trained model.
    A better extraction.

    2DGS learned a good model but exported broad phantom sheets of surface. The training photos already say where the object isn't, so depth outside each training mask was zeroed during extraction. No retraining. The fix was proven on the Lego, then carried to the boot unchanged.

    · Chapter IV · Case 03Footwear
    Case 03 · The pink boot

    One shoe,
    two roads.

    Photometric road · F 0.416

    Shape right, surface folded. The supplied normals disagree with the scan by ~11.8° (2.5× the bear's), and the boot fills under 7% of each frame. The bottleneck is input normals, not the integrator.

    Gaussian road · F 0.565 → 0.902

    GOF covered the boot (recall 0.98) but added extra surface (precision 0.40); half its triangles were inconsistently wound, traced to a Windows tetrahedra substitute. 2DGS with mask-gated extraction reached 0.902.

    Lesson
    For glossy footwear with good masks, Gaussians lead today. The photometric road needs normals from our own lights.

      · Appendix BHow we measure
      Five yardsticks

      The numbers, in plain words.

      Surface · higher is better

      F-score

      Share of the reconstruction near the scan (precision) and share of the scan covered (recall), combined. 0.90 ≈ nine in ten points agree.

      Surface · lower is better

      Chamfer

      Average distance from each surface to the other. Punishes stray floating pieces hard, which is why “cleaned” meshes improve so much.

      Outline · higher is better

      Silhouette IoU

      Render the mesh from a camera it never trained on and compare its outline with the real mask. Says nothing about depth or bumps.

      Normals · lower is better

      Angular error

      Per-pixel angle between predicted and scanned surface direction, averaged. Bear best: 2.4°. Boot inputs: about 11.8°.

      Cameras · lower is better

      Epipolar error

      Predicted cameras say where a matched point in one photo must fall in another: a line. Sampson error is the pixel distance from that line. Under 8 px passes.
